# No AI Writing: Patterns to Kill on Sight

A comprehensive checklist of AI writing patterns that must not appear in your company's blog posts. Adapted from Wikipedia's "Signs of AI Writing" guide, calibrated to a confident, specific company voice. Examples below use a fictional company ("Acme") and product ("Atlas") — substitute your own.

**When to read this file:** Before drafting any blog post (so you write around these patterns from the start), and again before finalizing (run the self-check checklist at the bottom). Finding one of these in a draft is a flag. Finding three or more means the piece needs a full rewrite, not a line edit.

The rules are organized into four buckets: **content patterns** (what the writing says), **language patterns** (the words it uses), **style patterns** (how it's formatted), and **structural patterns** (how it opens, closes, and transitions).

---

## A. Content Patterns

### A1. Puffery and hollow superlatives

AI inflates significance to fill space. Real writing earns its claims.

**Banned phrases:** testament to, hallmark of, pivotal moment, crucial development, transformative shift, groundbreaking achievement, paradigm shift.

**Bad:** "The launch of Atlas represents a pivotal moment in the evolution of cross-border payments, a testament to Acme's commitment to innovation."

**Fixed:** "Atlas launched in October. In its first quarter, partners moved $700M through it."

### A2. Superficial analysis pretending to be insight

AI attaches "-ing" phrases to facts to make them sound analyzed.

**Pattern:** `[fact], [highlighting/underscoring/reflecting/demonstrating] [vague claim].`

**Bad:** "Acme processed 10 million transactions last month, highlighting the platform's growing importance in the payments ecosystem."

**Why it's bad:** "Highlighting the growing importance" isn't a thought. What do those 10 million transactions actually mean?

**Fixed:** "Acme processed 10 million transactions last month. That's more than the previous three months combined."

**Watch for:** highlighting that, underscoring the importance of, reflecting the broader, demonstrating the commitment to, contributing to the growing, ensuring that, symbolizing the, marking a significant moment in, shaping the future of.

Rule: if you can delete the "-ing" phrase and the sentence loses nothing real, delete it.

### A3. Vague attribution

AI invents authority it doesn't have by attributing opinions to unnamed sources.

**Bad:** "Industry experts agree that instant payments are the future of cross-border transactions." / "Observers have noted Acme's growing role in the payments space." / "Several analysts have suggested that Atlas positions Acme favorably."

**Rule:** If you can't name the source, either cut the attribution entirely or make the claim in first person. "We believe X" is more honest and more confident than "experts suggest X."

### A4. The "despite challenges" formula

AI loves the rhetorical setup where a challenge is acknowledged only to be dismissed in the next clause.

**Banned constructions:**
- "Despite these challenges, the future looks bright..."
- "While obstacles remain, the trajectory is clear..."
- "Although questions persist, momentum continues to build..."

These structures pretend to engage with counterarguments without actually engaging. Either engage with the challenge specifically (with detail and stakes) or don't raise it.

### A5. Editorializing the reader's reaction

AI tells the reader what to think about the facts it just presented.

**Bad:** "This impressive milestone demonstrates the platform's remarkable capabilities."

**Fixed:** Just present the milestone. Let the reader draw the conclusion.

## B. Language Patterns

### B1. The AI vocabulary list

These words are statistically overrepresented in AI-generated writing. Seeing one occasionally is fine. A cluster signals the piece needs a full rewrite.

**Hard ban:**

| Word/phrase | Why it's a problem | Say instead |
|---|---|---|
| delve | Nobody says this in normal speech | explore, look at, get into |
| tapestry | Vague metaphor that means nothing | be specific about what you mean |
| testament to | Inflated significance | just say what the thing is |
| meticulous | Hollow positive | say what was actually done carefully |
| pivotal | Everything is pivotal; nothing is | say why it matters specifically |
| crucial | Same as pivotal | same fix |
| vibrant | Travel-brochure word | say what's actually happening |
| nestled | Travel-brochure word | — |
| boasts | Promotional, not factual | "has" |
| foster | Corporate softener | support, build, create |
| cultivate | Same as foster | — |
| showcase | "Show" is a word | show |
| leverage (as a verb) | Jargon | use |
| robust | Means nothing | say what makes it strong |
| seamless | Means nothing | say what's actually easy about it |
| cutting-edge | Means nothing | say what's actually new |
| innovative | Means nothing | say what's actually different |
| ecosystem (overused) | Catchall that avoids specificity | say what you actually mean |
| synergy / synergies | Means nothing | say what actually happens when two things combine |
| empower | Vague positive | say what people can actually do |
| transformative | Means nothing without evidence | say what actually changes |
| groundbreaking | See: game-changing | say what's actually new |
| landscape | "The payments landscape" means nothing | cut it or be specific |
| holistic | Means nothing | be specific |
| world-class | Means nothing | be specific |
| solutions | Empty noun | say what the thing is |
| in today's world / in today's fast-paced | Filler | cut entirely |
| it is worth noting | Filler — if it's worth noting, just note it | cut the preamble |
| it goes without saying | If it goes without saying, don't say it | cut entirely |
| in conclusion / in summary | Signals the end — readers know | cut it |

### B2. Synonym cycling (elegant variation)

AI avoids repeating the same word by substituting synonyms throughout a piece. The result sounds like it was written by a thesaurus.

**Bad:** "Acme launched Atlas last quarter. The platform has already attracted 12 enterprise partners. The solution handles cross-border payments. The product is now live in 14 markets."

"Platform," "solution," "product" — all meaning Atlas. This reads as AI avoiding repetition, not a human writing naturally.

**Fixed:** Pick the right word and use it consistently. If it's a product, call it the product. If it's Atlas, call it Atlas.

This pattern also damages referential cohesion — see the corpus targets in `SKILL.md`. Best-in-class blogs score higher on cohesion partly *because* they repeat key nouns. A low cohesion score partly reflects this exact synonym-cycling habit.

### B3. Negative parallelism ("not just X, but Y")

AI constructions that frame everything as correcting a misconception, even when no misconception exists.

**Bad:** "Acme isn't just a payments company — it's a complete financial infrastructure stack." / "This isn't just a speed improvement. It's a fundamental rethinking of what payments can be." / "Atlas isn't a product. It's a movement."

**Why it's bad:** This construction implies the reader had the wrong idea first. It's also a cliché — every AI-written tech piece uses it.

**Fixed:** Just say the real thing directly. "Acme is a complete financial infrastructure stack." / "This upgrade changes how payments work at a fundamental level."

### B4. Inflated copulas ("serves as," "stands as")

AI avoids simple constructions like "is" and "has" and replaces them with inflated alternatives. This makes sentences feel formal but actually less precise.

**Avoid:**
- serves as (→ is)
- stands as (→ is)
- acts as (→ is, often)
- represents (when you mean "is")
- functions as (→ is)
- operates as (→ is)

**Bad:** "Atlas serves as the premier infrastructure layer for global payments."
**Fixed:** "Atlas is the infrastructure layer for global payments."

### B5. The rule of three (overused)

Triads are a real rhetorical device. AI overuses them to pad thin points.

**Bad:** "Atlas is fast, reliable, and scalable." / "Acme is committed to security, efficiency, and innovation." / "Our platform is flexible, powerful, and easy to use."

**Why it's bad:** Three generic adjectives isn't a thought. If you can't say specifically what makes it fast, reliable, and scalable, the triad is hiding the absence of real content.

**Rule:** One specific claim beats three vague ones. If you use a triad, each element needs to be specific and distinct.

## C. Style Patterns

### C1. Em dashes — banned entirely

Em dashes (—) are one of the clearest signals of AI-generated writing. They are **not permitted in any blog content.** Not one, not as an aside, not for emphasis, not as a substitute for a comma or colon. Full stop.

**Why:** AI models reach for em dashes constantly because they create a false sense of rhythm and sophistication. In practice they interrupt sentence flow and make copy feel artificially constructed. Readers notice even if they can't name it.

**If you're tempted to use an em dash, use instead:**
- A period. Break it into two sentences.
- A comma, if the clause is genuinely subordinate.
- A colon, if you're introducing something.
- A parenthetical with actual parentheses, if it's truly an aside worth keeping.
- Nothing. Cut the aside entirely if it's not load-bearing.

**Bad:** "Atlas — built for enterprise payments — handles cross-border transactions in under 5 seconds."
**Fixed:** "Atlas is built for enterprise payments. It handles cross-border transactions in under five seconds."

**Also banned:** en dashes (–) used as em dashes. Hyphens between words are fine (cross-border, well-positioned). Anything longer than a hyphen used mid-sentence is not.

### C2. Boldface overuse

AI bolds nouns and phrases at random to perform emphasis. Real writing uses bold sparingly, and only where the reader genuinely needs to find the term again later (definitions, hard rules, key metrics).

**Bad:** Bold every product name, every metric, every "key" word. The post looks like a Christmas tree.
**Good:** Bold the term being defined on first use, the one or two hard numbers the reader will quote, the principle being asserted. That's it.

### C3. Title case in headers

AI defaults to Title Case For Every Header. Blog headers use sentence case (only the first word and proper nouns capitalized).

**Bad:** "How Acme Is Transforming Cross-Border Payments"
**Good:** "How Acme is changing cross-border payments"

### C4. Quotes around every term

AI scare-quotes terms it doesn't fully commit to. If you're using a term, commit to it.

**Bad:** "Acme's 'fast finality' delivers 'instant' settlement."
**Good:** "Acme delivers five-second finality. Settlement clears in the same block."

## D. Structural Patterns

### D1. The "In today's landscape" opener

Banned openers:
- "In today's rapidly evolving landscape..."
- "As the world becomes increasingly..."
- "In an era defined by..."
- "With the rise of..."
- "Against the backdrop of..."
- "In recent years, [topic] has emerged as..."

These are filler. They don't mean anything. Cut them and start with a real sentence — a hook, a claim, a story, a number.

### D2. The "In conclusion" closer

Banned closers:
- "In conclusion..."
- "In summary..."
- "Despite these challenges, the future looks bright..."
- "As [company] continues to grow, it remains committed to..."
- "Looking ahead, [company] is well-positioned to..."
- "Only time will tell..."

A good blog post ends with an implication or a forward motion, not a recap. The reader just read the post. They know what it said.

### D3. The didactic disclaimer

AI loves to caveat itself into invisibility, especially when it's about to say something interesting.

**Bad:** "It's important to acknowledge that there are many factors at play here, and reasonable people may disagree, but..."

**Fixed:** Just say the thing. If you have a real reservation, name it and engage with it. If you don't, drop the disclaimer.

### D4. Collaborative "we" with the reader (tour-guide framing)

AI addresses the reader as a co-explorer, which is condescending and reads as filler.

**Bad:** "In this post, we'll explore how Atlas works." / "Let's take a look at why fast finality matters." / "As we'll see, the implications are significant."

**Fixed:** Just say the thing. "Here's how Atlas works." "Fast finality matters because..." Drop the tour-guide framing.

Note: this is different from the editorial "we" the SKILL.md asks for. "We built Atlas to solve X" is the team narrating its own work. "Let's explore Atlas" is the AI narrating the reader's experience. The first is right. The second is banned.

### D5. The signposted thesis

AI announces what it's about to argue instead of just arguing it.

**Bad:** "This article will demonstrate that instant settlement is reshaping cross-border payments by examining three key factors..."

**Fixed:** Start with the strongest version of the argument. The reader will follow.

## Self-Check Checklist

Before finalizing any blog post, scan for every item below. Finding one is a flag. Finding three or more means the piece needs a full rewrite, not a line edit.

**Content:**
- [ ] Any puffery / "testament to" / "pivotal moment" language?
- [ ] Any "-ing" phrases attached to facts (highlighting, underscoring, reflecting)?
- [ ] Any vague unnamed attributions ("industry experts," "observers")?
- [ ] Any "despite challenges" / "while obstacles remain" formulas?
- [ ] Any editorializing the reader's reaction ("this impressive milestone")?

**Language:**
- [ ] Any words from the AI vocabulary list (delve, robust, seamless, cutting-edge, leverage, etc.)?
- [ ] Synonym cycling for the same noun (platform/solution/product all meaning the same thing)?
- [ ] "Not just X, but Y" constructions?
- [ ] "Serves as," "stands as," "acts as" instead of "is"?
- [ ] Vague triads (three generic adjectives in a row)?

**Style:**
- [ ] Any em dashes (—) or en dashes (–) used mid-sentence? (Banned entirely.)
- [ ] Boldface used decoratively instead of for definitions or hard numbers?
- [ ] Title case headers instead of sentence case?
- [ ] Scare quotes around terms the writer should just commit to?

**Structural:**
- [ ] "In today's landscape" / "As the world becomes" / "In an era" opener?
- [ ] "In conclusion" / "Only time will tell" / "Looking ahead" closer?
- [ ] Didactic disclaimers ("It's important to acknowledge...")?
- [ ] Tour-guide "we" ("Let's explore," "in this post we'll examine")?
- [ ] Signposted thesis ("This article will demonstrate that...")?

If the draft passes all 18 checks, it has cleared the AI-tells bar. It still needs to pass the corpus targets in `SKILL.md` (F-score, self-mention rate, booster-to-hedge ratio, referential cohesion) and the eight voice principles in `style-guide.md`.
